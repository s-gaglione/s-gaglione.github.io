The end of the navbar

If you can just ask for what you want, why would you ever open a menu? The question is more interesting than the obvious answer.

By Sullivan Gaglione · November 2026

The case for killing it

The navigation bar is a confession. It says: this product has more features than you can remember, so here is a table of contents. Every enterprise tool I have used grows the same way: a new feature, a new tab, a new submenu, until the navbar becomes a map nobody fully reads.

Conversation removes the need for the map. “Show me last quarter’s churned customers in Spain” skips four screens and two filters. For rare tasks, it is often faster: nobody remembers where the setting for fiscal year start lives, and nobody should have to.

So yes, in the products I expect to see in a few years, the classic left-hand menu with fifteen entries will look as dated as a toolbar with forty icons.

Chat is a door, not a room

Jakob Nielsen called this the first new interaction paradigm in sixty years: from telling the computer how to telling it what. The first products took it literally and put a chat box everywhere. Chat is excellent at capturing an intent that fits no existing button. It is poor at what follows: reviewing twelve invoices in a scrolling transcript is worse than in a table, and retyping a paragraph to change one parameter is worse than moving a slider. The better pattern is a short exchange in language, then a return to direct manipulation.

What conversation is bad at

But a navbar does three jobs that conversation does badly.

  • It shows what is possible. A blank input asks the user to already know what to ask. Menus teach the product. Without them, most users never discover half of what the software can do.
  • It shows state at a glance. A badge with “3 invoices to approve” needs no question. Pull-based interfaces make people ask; good interfaces tell them.
  • It rewards habit. The tenth time you do something, you don’t want to describe it. You want one click in the same place as yesterday.

Recognition beats recall: that principle is older than the web, and language models do not repeal it.

What replaces it

I don’t think the navbar disappears. I think it changes what it lists. Today it lists features, the places where the product’s capabilities live. Tomorrow it will list outcomes: the things you asked for, the views you kept, the workspaces you return to, the tasks waiting on you.

Picture three zones. An input, always available, for intent. A canvas, where the result appears as a real interface: table, form, chart, draft. And a rail that remembers: pinned results, recurring requests turned into one-click shortcuts, items that need attention. The navigation is no longer designed once by a product team. It is assembled from what each person actually does.

What this asks of product teams

  1. Design for discoverability without menus. Suggested requests, contextual hints and examples become the new onboarding.
  2. Turn repeated conversations into buttons. When someone asks the same thing three times, offer to pin it. Personal shortcuts beat generic menus.
  3. Keep a source of truth for state. Notifications, counters and statuses still need a fixed home. The assistant should be able to explain them, not replace them.
  4. Make everything reachable both ways. Every capability should be callable by language and reachable by direct manipulation. Some days people talk; some days they click.

So, the end?

The end of the navbar as a table of contents, yes. Its replacement is a navigation that is lighter, personal and partly generated, sitting next to a conversation that does the heavy lifting. The products that win will not be the ones that remove every menu. They will be the ones where you never have to look for one.
