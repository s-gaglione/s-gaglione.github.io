Design note

One product, a thousand interfaces

We have always designed one interface for an imaginary average user. AI makes it possible to adapt the interface to each real one. That changes accessibility, onboarding and what “consistent” even means.

By Sullivan Gaglione · November 2026

The end of the average user

Every product team designs for personas: the accountant, the manager, the occasional user. Then everyone gets the same screens, and the expert finds them slow while the newcomer finds them overwhelming. Settings and “advanced mode” toggles were the best we could do.

When an interface can be partly assembled at runtime, it can respond to the person in front of it: denser for the expert, more guided for the newcomer, simpler for someone on a phone in a hurry. Not by guessing a persona, but by observing what this person does and asking what they need.

Accessibility on demand

This is where adaptation helps most, provided you check what it removes: a simplification can drop an important condition. Larger text and higher contrast are old features. AI adds new ones: rewriting a dense paragraph at a simpler reading level, translating the interface and its content into the user’s language, describing a chart for someone who cannot see it, turning a long form into a short conversation for someone who struggles with forms. Many people who were poorly served by one-size-fits-all interfaces can now get one that fits.

The consistency problem

Adaptation has a price. Interfaces work because they are predictable: the button is where it was yesterday, the colleague sees the same screen you do, the support agent can say “click on Settings, then Billing”. An interface that rearranges itself for each person breaks all three.

The answer, I think, is to separate what may adapt from what must stay fixed. Navigation landmarks, terminology, the position of critical actions and anything legal should be stable. Density, guidance, language, examples and the order of secondary content can adapt. Users should always be able to see that something was adapted, and to switch back.

Onboarding disappears into the product

Product tours and tooltips are an admission that the interface does not explain itself. An adaptive interface can teach in context: the first time someone hesitates on a feature, it offers the one sentence they need, then gets out of the way as they become fluent. The interface grows with the user instead of the user having to grow into the interface.

Principles I would start from

  1. Adapt content and density, keep the skeleton stable.
  2. Make adaptation visible and reversible. “Simplified for you · show full view.”
  3. Ask before inferring. A one-question preference beats a silent guess.
  4. Test with the extremes. The expert, the newcomer, the screen-reader user and the person on a slow phone, not just the average.
